### Example 9-26

If clients are not completely satisfied with his landscaping work, Ramon offers a $200 refund with no questions asked. Near the end of 2024, four clients requested refunds,

---

so the liability for the refunds was incurred in 2024. However, Ramon was busy during the holiday season, so he didn’t pay the refunds until January 2025. Ignoring the recurring item exception, when can Ramon deduct the customer refunds under the cash and accrual methods? Answer: Because refunds are payment liabilities, under the accrual method economic performance does not occur until Ramon pays the refunds. Consequently, the $800 of refunds are deductible in 2025 even though the liability for the refunds met the all-events test in 2024. Under the cash method, Ramon would not deduct the refunds until he paid them in 2025. What if: Suppose that Ramon received $800 of refund requests at year-end. Under what conditions could Ramon deduct the refunds in 2024 if he uses accrual accounting and elects to use the recurring item exception? Answer: There are four conditions to meet the recurring item exception. First, the liability must meet the all-events test and, second, be recurring in nature. Third, either the accrued expense must either be immaterial in amount or a better match to current revenue than to future revenue. Finally, economic performance (payment) must occur within a reasonable time after year-end (but not longer than 8½ months or the filing of the tax return). Ramon’s refunds meet the first two conditions and he doesn’t need to worry about the matching requirement because regulations deem refunds to meet the matching requirement. Since the refunds are paid shortly after year-end, Ramon meets the final requirement. Note that once the recurring item exception is elected for refunds, Ramon must follow this method of accounting for refunds in future periods.

Bad Debt Expense When accrual-method businesses sell a product or a service on credit, they debit accounts receivable and credit sales revenue for both financial and tax purposes. However, when a business is unable to collect the full amount of its accounts receivable, it incurs bad debt expense (a customer owes them a debt that the customer will not or cannot pay). For financial reporting purposes, the business estimates the amount of the bad debt at year-end and creates a reserve account, the allowance for doubtful accounts. However, for tax purposes, businesses are only allowed to deduct bad debt expense when the debt actually becomes worthless within the taxable year.54 Consequently, for tax purposes, when businesses determine which specific debts are uncollectible, they are entitled to a deduction. This method of determining bad debt expense for tax purposes is called the direct write-off method. In contrast, the method used for financial reporting purposes is called the allowance method. As an aside, businesses using the cash method of accounting are not allowed to deduct bad debt expenses because they do not include associated receivables in taxable income.

At year-end, Ramon estimates that about $900 of the receivables for his services will be uncollectible, but he has identified only one client, Jared, who will definitely not pay his bill. Jared, who has skipped town, owes Ramon $280 for landscaping this fall. What amount of bad debt expense will Ramon recognize for financial purposes, and what amount bad debt expense may Ramon deduct for the year under the accrual and cash methods? Answer: For financial reporting purposes, Ramon would recognize a $900 bad debt expense. However, for tax purposes, the direct write-off method must be used, and under this method Ramon can only deduct $280—the amount associated with specifically writing off Jared’s receivable. Under the cash method, Ramon would not be able to claim any deduction because under the cash method he did not record a receivable from Jared and thus has not recognized income on the sale to Jared.

Limitations on Accruals An accrual-method business generally deducts compensation expense as employees perform services. However, a business is not allowed to deduct compensation accrued at year-end unless it actually pays the compensation within 2½ months of year-end. This 2½-month rule applies primarily to deferred compensation such as year-end bonuses and vacation pay. Another limitation applies to payments made to related persons. Tax law prohibits an accrual-method business from accruing (and deducting) an expense for a liability owed to a related person who uses the cash method until such time that the related person recognizes the income associated with the payment.55 This requirement limits the ability of a business to deduct an accrued expense until the period when the recipient is taxed on the income. For this purpose, related persons include: Page 9-28

Family members, including parents, siblings, and spouses. Shareholders and C corporations when the shareholder owns more than 50 percent of the corporation’s stock.56 Owners of partnerships and S corporations no matter the ownership percentage.57

This issue frequently arises in situations in which a business using the accrual method employs the owner or a relative of an owner. The business is not allowed to deduct compensation expense owed to the related person until the year in which the related person includes the compensation in income. However, this related-person limit extends beyond compensation to any accrued expense the business owes to a related cash-method taxpayer.
