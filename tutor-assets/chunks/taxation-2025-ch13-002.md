## EMPLOYER-PROVIDED QUALIFIED PLANS

Traditional qualified retirement plans grew from a congressional concern that, if left unregulated, employers might not adequately fund and protect employee retirement benefits and might be tempted to provide lavish retirement plans to highly compensated employees such as executives and business owners to the detriment of rank-and-file employees. To address these concerns, Congress mandated that employer-provided plans receive tax-favored qualified plan status only if they meet certain requirements, thus ensuring that the plan does not discriminate against rank-and-file employees and promised benefits are secure. Over the years, the rules addressing these issues have evolved, but the concept of a qualified plan eligible for favorable tax treatment remains the same. Employer-provided qualified plans can be generally classified as defined benefit plans or defined contribution plans. As the name suggests, defined benefit plans spell out the specific benefit the employee will receive upon retirement. Because the defined benefit plan specifies the amount of payments (i.e., the plan defines the benefit to be received by the employee) the employer will pay to the employee in retirement (usually a percentage of their salary based on the number of years the employee worked for the employer), a defined benefit plan is commonly referred to as a pension plan. In contrast, defined contribution plans specify the maximum annual contributions that employers will contribute to the plan and the amount that employees may contribute to the plan. A common defined contribution plan is a 401(k) plan. While we explore both basic plan types below, we emphasize defined contribution plans because they are much more common than defined benefit plans. Later in the chapter, we describe and discuss non-employer-provided plans such as IRAs and retirement accounts for self-employed taxpayers such as SEP IRAs.

## DEFINED BENEFIT PLANS

### LO 13-1

Defined benefit plans are traditional pension plans used by many older and more established companies and tax-exempt organizations. These plans provide standard retirement benefits to employees based on a fixed formula. The formula to determine an employee’s standard retirement benefit is usually a function of years of service working for the employer sponsoring the plan and the level of the employee’s compensation as they near retirement. For example, an employer may offer a defined benefit plan that pays retiring employees 2 percent for each full year of service, up to a maximum of 60 percent (i.e., 30 years of service), of the average of the employee’s three highest consecutive calendar years of compensation. However, the amount of compensation used in calculating the maximum retirement benefit allowed under the tax law for a particular year is restricted to the annual compensation limit for that year. Consequently, if a retiring employee’s compensation for a year is above the limit for that particular year, the annual limit amount is used in the retirement benefit calculation. The annual compensation limit is $290,000 for 2021, $305,000 for 2022, $330,000 for 2023, and $345,000 for 2024.2 For employees who begin receiving retirement benefits in 2024, the maximum annual benefit they can receive is the lesser of (1) 100 percent of the average of the employee’s three highest consecutive calendar years of compensation, limited to the annual compensation limitation for each of the three years, or (2) $275,000.3

### Example 13-1

Corporate Business Associates (CBA) provides a defined benefit plan for its employees. The plan uses a fixed formula to determine employee benefits. The formula specifies a benefit of 3 percent for each year of service, up to a maximum of 90 percent (30 years of service), of the average of the employee’s three highest consecutive calendar years of compensation up to the annual compensation limitation for each year. In January of 2024, Jason retired from CBA. On the date of retirement, Jason had been a CBA employee for 20 years. His highest three consecutive calendar years of annual compensation were $420,000 in 2021, $450,000 in 2022, and $480,000 in 2023. What is Jason’s annual benefit from the defined benefit plan that he will begin receiving in 2024? Answer: $185,000 a year. The benefit is calculated by multiplying 60 percent (3 percent for each of his 20 years of service) by $308,333 (the average of his highest three years of salary, limited to the annual compensation limit of $290,000 for 2021, $305,000 for 2022, and $330,000 for 2023 because his actual compensation is higher than the limit for each of these three years).
