A QCD is a distribution from a traditional IRA (not a Roth or a SEP IRA) made directly by the IRA trustee to a qualified charitable organization.28 The benefit of a QCD is that it counts as a distribution for required minimum distribution (RMD) purposes, but the distribution is excluded from the taxpayer’s gross income (because it is excluded from income, the charitable donation is not deductible as a charitable contribution).29 A distribution can qualify as a QCD only to the extent the distribution would have been included in gross income before considering the QCD exclusion (i.e., distributions of nondeductible contributions do not count as QCDs). The QCD annual limit is $105,000 per IRA owner (indexed for inflation). Therefore, each spouse could complete a $105,000 QCD for married couples filing jointly. The QCD provides an alternative to a taxpayer receiving a taxable distribution from the IRA and then donating the funds to a charitable organization. Therefore, a QCD would benefit older taxpayers who regularly make charitable donations but do not have enough other itemized deductions in total to gain full tax savings from the charitable donations itemized deductions. A taxpayer completing a QCD would also report a lower AGI than they would without the QCD. Consequently, the taxpayer would receive tax benefits from the QCD for tax items that are phased out or reduced based on AGI (e.g., floor for medical expense deductions, taxability of social security income, threshold for net investment income tax, etc.). By making a QCD, the taxpayer effectively converts a potential itemized deduction for the charitable donation (from AGI deduction) into a for AGI deduction because the QCD reduces gross income (and therefore AGI) from what it would have been if the taxpayer had received the distribution without the QCD. When a taxpayer receives a distribution from a traditional IRA to which nondeductible contributions have been made, the taxpayer is taxed on the earnings generated by the nondeductible contributions but not on the actual nondeductible contributions. When taxpayers receive partial distributions from an IRA to which they have made nondeductible contributions (i.e., they have basis in the IRA), each distribution consists of a taxable and a nontaxable component (the nontaxable component is the portion that comes out of the taxpayer’s IRA basis). Roth IRAs As an alternative to a traditional IRA, taxpayers meeting certain requirements can contribute to a Roth IRA. Contributions to a Roth IRA are not deductible, and qualifying distributions from a Roth IRA are not taxable.

## THE KEY FACTS

### Roth IRAs

Contributions are not deductible.

Same contribution limits as traditional IRAs but

For 2024, contribution phases out for MAGI between $146,000 and $161,000 for unmarried taxpayers and between $230,000 and $240,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly.

Qualified distributions are not taxable.

To qualify, account must be open for at least five years before distribution and distribution must be made after taxpayer reaches 59½ years of age (among others).

Nonqualified distributions of account earnings

Taxed at ordinary rates and subject to 10 percent penalty if the taxpayer is not at least 59½ years of age at the time of the distribution.

Distributions come first from contributions and then from account earnings.

Contributions Roth IRAs are subject to the same annual contribution limits as traditional IRAs [the lesser of $7,000 ($8,000 including the $1,000 catch-up contribution if taxpayer is at least age 50 at year-end) or earned income]. Further, the same spousal IRA rules that apply to traditional IRAs apply to Roth IRA contributions. These limits apply to the sum of a taxpayer’s contributions to deductible IRAs, nondeductible IRAs, and Roth IRAs. Whether taxpayers participate in an employer-sponsored retirement plan or not, the Roth IRA contribution limitation phases out based on modified AGI (MAGI) as follows:31 Unmarried taxpayers No phase-out if the taxpayer’s MAGI is $146,000 or below. Proportional phase-out of full limit for MAGI between $146,000 and $161,000. Full phase-out (no contribution) if MAGI is $161,000 or higher.
