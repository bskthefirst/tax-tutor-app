If taxpayer does not participate in employer-provided plan but spouse does

Nondeductible Contributions Taxpayers whose maximum deductible contribution is phased out based on MAGI can still make nondeductible contributions up to the overall contribution limit. Further, taxpayers who are able to deduct IRA contributions can designate otherwise deductible contributions as nondeductible. When a taxpayer makes a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA during the year, the taxpayer must file Form 8606, “Nondeductible IRAs,” to inform the IRS of the amount of the taxpayer’s nondeductible contributions for the year. Nondeductible contributions create basis in the taxpayer’s IRA that is not taxed on distribution. The overall limit of the sum of deductible and nondeductible contributions (including Roth IRAs—see below) is $7,000 per year ($8,000 including the $1,000 catch-up contribution for taxpayers who are at least 50 years of age at year-end). Earned income limitations (including the spousal IRA limits) that apply to deductible IRA contributions also apply to nondeductible contributions. That is, a taxpayer must have earned income in order to make nondeductible contributions to a traditional IRA, with the exception of a spouse who is contributing to a spousal IRA. The earnings on nondeductible contributions grow tax-deferred until the taxpayer receives distributions from the IRA (see discussion below). Thus, most taxpayers making nondeductible contributions to traditional IRAs would likely do better by contributing directly to a Roth IRA, if eligible, instead of making nondeductible contributions to traditional IRAs. Another alternative would be transferring the nondeductible contributions into a Roth IRA (termed a “backdoor” Roth IRA contribution). We discuss Roth IRAs after discussing traditional IRAs. Taxpayers may contribute to an IRA up until the unextended tax return due date (generally, April 15 of the subsequent year). That is, as long as the taxpayer makes a contribution to the IRA by April 15 of year 2, the contribution counts as though it were made during year 1 (the prior calendar year). However, when a taxpayer makes a year 1 contribution in year 2, the taxpayer needs to specify to the IRA custodian that the contribution is intended to be a year 1 rather than a year 2 contribution.
