The Gross Estate Property possessed by or owned (titled) by a decedent at the time of death is generally referred to as the probate estate because the transfer of this property is carried out by a probate court. Probate is the process of gathering property possessed by or titled in the name of a decedent at the time of death, paying the debts of the decedent, and transferring the ownership of any remaining property to the decedent’s heirs. Property in the probate estate can include cash, stocks, jewelry, clothing, and realty owned by or titled in the name of the deceased at the time of death. The person appointed by the court to carry out the will is called the executor. If the decedent does not have a valid will at the time of death, the person is said to die intestate. The gross estate is broader than the probate estate. The gross estate consists of (1) the fair market value of property possessed or owned by a decedent at death plus (2) the value of certain automatic property transfers that take effect at death.14 Property transfers that take effect only at death are not in the probate estate because the transfer takes place just as death occurs. Hence, the probate court does not need to effect a transfer. Establishing a trust to hold the title to property is another method of

avoiding probate. The ownership of property transferred to these living trusts automatically vests in a designated beneficiary upon the occurrence of an event, such as death of the donor.

### Example 25-12

Nate took an inventory of his father’s property in preparation for distributing assets according to Juan’s will. Nate must report this preliminary inventory of personal and investment property to the probate court. Juan’s property includes the following:

| Property | Fair Market Value |
| --- | --- |
| Auto | $ 33,000 |
| Clothes, furniture, and personal effects | 48,000 |
| Smith painting (original cost/initial estimated value) | 25,000 |
| Checking and savings accounts | 75,250 |
| FFP stock | 19,500,000 |
| Residence | 400,000 |
| Other investments | 8,200 |
| Total | $20,089,450 |

What amount of this property is included in Juan’s gross estate? Answer: Juan’s gross estate includes the value of all the above property, or $20,089,450, because he owned these assets at his death (i.e., these assets are included in Juan’s probate estate). What if: Suppose Juan was also entitled to a pension distribution of $15,000 but had not yet received the check at the time of his death. Would this value also be included in Juan’s probate estate and therefore gross estate? Answer: Yes. Although Juan had not received the check, he was legally entitled to the property at the time of his death, and therefore it will be included in both his probate estate and his gross estate.

## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

When does an estate include a check written prior to the decedent’s death? In January 2007, William DeMuth granted his son Donald a power of attorney and authorized him to make gifts to his brothers and other family members. Pursuant to this grant, Donald made annual gifts that individually did not exceed the annual exclusion. In the summer of 2015, William’s health began to deteriorate, and by early September, William was near death. On September 6, Donald wrote 11 checks, totaling $464,000, from his father’s investment account. William passed away on September 11, but only 1 of

---

the 11 checks was paid before William’s death. Three of the checks were deposited by the payees on September 11 but were not paid until September 14. The remaining checks, totaling $366,000, were deposited after September 11 and paid on September 15. Donald was the executor of William’s estate and filed an estate tax return that excluded the value of all 11 checks. Because the gifts were below the annual exclusion, none of the gifts were subject to gift tax. Upon audit, the IRS argued that the September gifts were incomplete because under Pennsylvania law a stop payment order would have allowed the donor to recall the payments. Since William’s bank did not pay the 10 checks in question until after William’s death, the IRS argued that 10 of the 11 checks should be includible in William’s gross estate. The Tax Court agreed with this argument, but noted that prior to the hearing, the IRS had stipulated that three of the checks had been “accepted" by William’s bank. In reality, these three checks had only been deposited in the payee’s bank and not paid by William’s bank. Hence, despite being signed, delivered, and endorsed by the donees, seven checks representing $366,000 were still included in William’s estate because the checks were not paid before Donald’s death. Source: Estate of DeMuth v. Comm’r, TC Memo 2022-72.

Specific Inclusions Besides property in the probate estate, the gross estate also includes property transferred automatically at the decedent’s death. These automatic transfers can occur without the help of a probate court because the ownership transfers by law at the time of death. For example, property can be transferred automatically through a living trust. Certain automatic property transfers are specifically included in the gross estate because, while the decedent didn’t own the property at death, they controlled the ultimate disposition of the property. That is, the decedent effectively determined who would receive the property at the time of death. Joint ownership of property is the most common form of automatic transfer, but the amounts included in the gross estate vary with the form of joint ownership. Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship legally transfers to the surviving tenant upon the joint tenant’s death. For example, joint bank accounts are commonly owned in joint ownership with right of survivorship. In contrast, tenants in common hold divided rights to property and have the ability to transfer these rights during their life or upon death. Property held by tenants in common, such as real estate, does not automatically transfer at death and thus must be transferred via probate. Although the decedent’s interest in jointly owned property (with the right of survivorship) ceases at death, the value of the interest the decedent held in this property is still included in the gross estate.15
