### Example 16-6

What if: Assume that on January 1, 2022, PCC issued 10,000 incentive stock options (ISOs) with an estimated fair value of $6 per option. The options vest at the end of four years. For 2024, PCC records a $15,000 compensation expense related to the ISOs (10,000 options × $6/4 years). What is PCC’s 2024 book–tax difference associated

with the incentive stock options? Would the difference be favorable or unfavorable? Would the difference be permanent or temporary? Answer: $15,000 unfavorable, permanent difference. PCC does not deduct compensation expense related to the ISOs for tax purposes.

---

Nonqualified options (NQOs) can generate both temporary and permanent book–tax differences. Corporations recognize unfavorable book–tax difference each year equal to the value of the options that vest during the year but are not exercised (book deduction but no tax deduction). The unfavorable book–tax differences completely reverse (corporations recognize a favorable temporary book–tax difference equal to the previous unfavorable book–tax differences) in the year the employee exercises the stock options. When an employee exercises an NQO, the tax deduction, which is the difference between the fair market value of the stock purchased and the exercise price on the exercise date, likely will differ from the compensation expense recorded on the income statement over the requisite service period (i.e., the estimated value of the options for book purposes). If the tax deduction exceeds the total book expense for the options, the corporation recognizes a favorable permanent book–tax difference in the amount of the excess. Conversely, if the total book expense for the options exceeds the tax deduction, the corporation recognizes an unfavorable permanent book–tax difference in the amount of the excess.

What if: Assume that on January 1, 2024, PCC granted 20,000 NQOs with an estimated $10 fair value per option ($200,000 total fair value). Each option entitled the recipient to purchase one share of PCC stock for $10 a share (the per-share price of PCC stock on January 1, 2024, when the options were granted). The options vested at the end of the day on December 31, 2025. Assume no options were exercised in 2024 or 2025. For 2024 and 2025, PCC deducts compensation expense of $100,000 for book purposes. What are PCC’s book–tax differences associated with the nonqualified options in 2024 and 2025? Identify each difference as favorable or unfavorable and permanent or temporary. Answer: $100,000 unfavorable, temporary book–tax difference in both 2024 and 2025. PCC reports $100,000 of compensation expense for book purposes in 2024 and 2025 and $0 for tax purposes in both years (no options were exercised in 2024 or 2025). What if: Assume the same facts as above and that on March 1, 2026, employees exercised all 20,000 options at a time when the PCC stock was trading at $25 per share. What is PCC’s book–tax difference associated with the stock options in 2026? Identify the difference as favorable or unfavorable and permanent or temporary. Answer: $200,000 favorable, temporary book–tax difference in 2026 and a $100,000 favorable permanent book–tax difference. PCC claims a $300,000 tax deduction in 2026, equal to the number of shares purchased (20,000) times the bargain element of $15 per option exercised ($25 − $10). PCC does not deduct any additional compensation expense for book purposes in 2026 because the vesting period ended in 2025. $200,000 of the tax deduction is a reversal of the total 2024–2025 unfavorable, temporary book–tax differences. The excess $100,000 tax deduction is a favorable, permanent difference.

## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

Meta’s NQO Tax Benefits: What a difference a year makes! The Meta (Facebook) stock price increased by about 27 percent during 2021 and then decreased by about 64 percent in 2022. Besides affecting the Meta shareholders (for better or worse), the changing stock price also affected Meta’s tax expense as reported in its financial statements, due to its impact on the amount of Meta’s tax deduction for its share-based compensation (e.g., stock options). Recall that for tax purposes, when Meta exercises its stock options, it deducts the difference between the stock price and the exercise price. Thus, the higher the stock price, the bigger the tax deduction. When the stock price is below the exercise price (i.e., the options are under water), there is no tax deduction, even though there would be a book expense (valued differently) for share-based compensation. For 2021, when the stock price was increasing, Meta reported excess tax benefits of $1.13 billion (i.e., the favorable permanent book–tax difference for stock options multiplied by 21 percent is $1.13 billion). In contrast, for 2022, when the stock prices was declining, Meta reported a tax shortfall of $471 million (i.e., the unfavorable permanent book-tax difference for stock options multiplied by 21 percent is $471 million). Thus, Meta’s declining stock price in 2022 resulted in Meta owing more than $1.5 billion dollars more in taxes in 2022 than in 2021 (compared to the taxes they would have owed if they had been able to deduct the book expense on their tax return). That’s a double whammy! Source: Meta’s Form 10K for 2021 and 2022 (both are publicly available).
