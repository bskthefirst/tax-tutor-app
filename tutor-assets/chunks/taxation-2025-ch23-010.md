## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

### Cryptocurrency Income Implications

The first cryptocurrency (Bitcoin) was mined in 2009 and had a value of only a few cents. By 2023, market estimates of global cryptocurrency were $1.7 Trillion. The staggering growth of cryptocurrency has brought increased government attention, both domestic and internationally, to regulate and tax this new market. For federal income tax purposes, there are multiple ways that income can be derived from crypto activities. For those working in the crypto industry, such as mining (a service performed that creates new “virtual” coins), the value of the new crypto received as payment for those services will be taxed as ordinary income. Similarly, when a business accepts crypto in exchange for goods and services it provides, the value of the crypto received will be treated as ordinary income, just as if cash had been accepted. However, IRS Notice 2014-21 announced that the United States will treat cryptocurrency as property rather than fiat currency. This property designation creates an additional taxable income event when the crypto received in these transactions is subsequently

---

disposed of—this taxable income event does not occur when traditional currency is used. The taxpayer recognizes a capital gain or loss for the difference between the value and basis, just like other property transactions. There are many more complex crypto transactions, some are non-taxable, but the majority are taxable. For an excellent primer on taxability of crypto see Taxbit’s cryptocurrency tax guide located here: http s://taxbit.com/cryptocurrency-tax-guide. Government regulation, including information disclosure, has become a reality for the industry. Since 2019, the IRS has been asking taxpayers whether they had disposed of a digital asset. In 2021, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act substantially expanded the definition and number of parties (digital asset brokers, exchanges, and marketplaces) required to file 1099s with the IRS beginning in January 2023. While federal tax laws are becoming clearer, most states take years to address how to integrate federal laws into state statute. In 2023, 17 states claim to conform to federal treatment and seven do not conform, the remaining states have remained silent. However, many important state issues relating to cryptocurrency remain unresolved. One important question is whether cryptocurrency related income is business or nonbusiness income? When it is considered to be business income, the income will be apportioned among the states where the business files; Otherwise, the income will be allocated to the state of commercial domicile. The question is important because if some states treat the income as business income while other states treat the income as nonbusiness income, then opportunities for double taxation exist. If cryptocurrency related income is business income, another question is whether the income is included or excluded from business receipts. States occasionally exclude income from business receipts (like income from hedging and other similar transactions) to prevent distortion in the apportionment of business income. Cryptocurrency rules, for both federal and state taxes, will continue to evolve as cryptocurrency transactions become more mainstream in the business and investing communities.

Rents and royalties are generally allocated to the state where the property is used.

All state taxable income is taxed in the state of commercial domicile unless the business is taxable in more than one state. An interstate business must separate its business income (earned from business operations) from nonbusiness income (primarily from investments, including rents and royalties). The business must fairly apportion its business income among the states in which it conducts business in a manner that creates income tax filing requirements, whereas it allocates or assigns nonbusiness income to a specific state (usually the state of commercial domicile).32

Business Income Business income includes all revenues earned in the ordinary course of business—sales less cost of goods sold and other expenses. Business income is fairly apportioned or divided across the states with an income tax filing requirement.33 If a business has income tax nexus or a filing requirement with more than one state, it has the right to apportion income to that state—even if the state does not actually impose a tax (e.g., Wyoming).34
