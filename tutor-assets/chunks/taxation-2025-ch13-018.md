### Self-Employed Retirement Accounts

Popular plans include SEP IRAs and individual 401(k)s.

Similar to qualified defined contribution plans.

We’ve discussed retirement savings opportunities available to employees. However, many taxpayers are self-employed small business owners who do not have access to employer-sponsored plans.39 Moreover, individually managed retirement plans such as traditional and Roth IRAs are not particularly attractive to self-employed taxpayers due to the relatively low contribution limits on these plans. What retirement savings options, then, are available to the self-employed? Congress has created a number of retirement savings plans for self-employed taxpayers. Two of the more popular are simplified employee pension (SEP) IRAs and individual (or “self-employed” or “solo”) 401(k) plans. These are defined contribution plans that generally work the same way as employer-provided plans. That is, amounts set aside in these plans are deducted from income, earnings are free of tax until distributed, and distributions from the plans are fully taxable.40 As we discuss below, these plans differ in terms of their annual contribution limits. They also have different nontax characteristics, including their suitability for businesses with employees other than the owner and their administration costs. As you might expect, these factors should be considered when self-employed small business owners choose a retirement plan.41 We describe SEP IRAs and individual 401(k) plans below.42

Simplified Employee Pension (SEP) IRA A simplified employee pension (SEP) can be administered through an individual retirement account (IRA) called a SEP IRA.43 The owner of a sole proprietorship can make annual contributions directly to a SEP IRA. For 2024, the annual contribution is limited to the lesser of: $69,000 or 20 percent of Schedule C net income, after reducing Schedule C net income by the deduction for the employer’s portion of self-employment taxes paid (50 percent of the self-employment taxes paid).44

In general, the self-employment tax base is 92.35 percent of Schedule C net income (termed “net earnings from self-employment”). The self-employment tax rate is 15.3 percent of the first $168,600 of self employment tax base plus 2.9 percent on the amount above $168,600 (2024 limit). See the

Contributions can be made up to the extended due date of the tax return.45 Beginning in 2023, taxpayers are allowed to make nondeductible contributions to a Roth SEP IRA instead of (or in addition to) a traditional SEP IRA. The annual contribution limit applies to the sum of contributions to both Roth and traditional SEP IRAs.

Lesser of (1) $69,000 or (2) 20 percent of Schedule C net income (after reducing Schedule C net income by the deduction for the employer’s portion of self-employment taxes paid).

If a sole proprietor has hired employees, the sole proprietor must contribute to the employees’ respective SEP IRAs based on their compensation. Because owners may view this requirement as being too costly, the SEP IRA is best suited for sole proprietors who do not have employees or who are willing to provide generous benefits to their employees. From an administrative perspective, SEP IRAs are easy to set up and have relatively low administrative costs from year to year.

Individual 401(k) Plans THE KEY FACTS Self-Employed Retirement Accounts

Individual 401(k) 2024 contribution limit.

Lesser of (1) $69,000 or (2) 20 percent of Schedule C net income (after reducing Schedule C net income by the deduction for the employer’s portion of self-employment taxes paid) plus $23,000 The additional $23,000 is allowed as an “employee” contribution.

Taxpayers who are at least 50 years old at the end of the year may contribute an additional $7,500 per year (maximum of $76,500, if self-employment earnings are sufficient). The additional contribution limit is considered to be an “employee contribution.”

Contribution cannot exceed Schedule C net income minus self-employment tax deduction.

Individual 401(k) plans are strictly for sole proprietors (and their spouses who work for the business) who do not have employees. Under this type of plan, for 2024, the sole proprietor can generally contribute the lesser of: $69,000 ($76,500 if at least 50 years old at year-end) or 20 percent of Schedule C net income, after reducing Schedule C net income by the deduction for the employer’s portion of self-employment taxes paid (50 percent of self-employment taxes paid) plus an additional $23,000 (employee’s contribution).

Further, if the sole proprietor is at least 50 years of age by the end of the tax year, the proprietor may contribute an additional $7,500 as a catch-up contribution. Thus, a self-employed taxpayer with sufficient self-employment earnings who is at least 50 years of age at year-end could contribute up to $76,500 to an individual 401(k) for 2024 ($69,000 + $7,500). The overall limit consists of $23,000 ($30,500 if 50 years of age at year-end) of “employee” contributions (note that SEP IRAs don’t have employee contributions) and employer contributions of $69,000 (or $76,500 if age 50 or over) minus the employee contributions. Taxpayers can set up an individual Roth 401(k) to receive employee contributions and employer contributions (individual Roth 401(k) employer contributions are allowed beginning in 2024).
