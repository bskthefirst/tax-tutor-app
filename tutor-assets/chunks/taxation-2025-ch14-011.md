Residence with Significant Rental Use (Vacation Home) When a home qualifies as a residence and the taxpayer rents out the home for 15 days or more, rental revenue is included in gross income, expenses to obtain tenants (i.e., advertising and commissions for real estate agents) are fully deductible as direct rental expenses, and expenses relating to the home are allocated between personal and rental use. The expenses allocated to personal use are not deductible except as permitted as itemized deductions under nonrental tax provisions. (These deductions are reported on Schedule A of Form 1040 and include mortgage interest, real property taxes, and certain casualty losses on the home.) All other expenses allocated to personal use are not deductible. Expenses are generally allocated to rental use based on the ratio of the number of days of rental use to the total number of days the property was used for rental and personal purposes. All expenses not allocated to rental use are allocated to personal use. The only potential exception to the general allocation rule is the allocation of mortgage interest expense and real property taxes. The IRS and the Tax Court disagree on how to allocate these particular expenses. Under the IRS allocation method, these expenses are allocated the same way as all other expenses. However, under the Tax Court allocation method, interest and taxes are allocated to rental use based on the ratio of days the property was rented over the number of days in the year, rather than the number of days the property was used for any purpose during the year.19 The IRS and Tax Court allocation methods are described in Exhibit 14-4.

## THE KEY FACTS

### Rental Use of the Home

Residence with significant rental use.

Rental use is 15 days or more and personal use exceeds the greater of (1) 14 days or (2) 10 percent of rental days.

Deduct direct rental expenses such as advertising and commissions for real estate agents.

Allocate home-related expenses between rental use and personal use.

Interest and taxes allocated to personal use are deducted as itemized deductions (subject to relevant limitations); all other expenses allocated to personal use are not deductible.

IRS method allocates interest and taxes to rental use based on rental use to total use for the year.

Tax Court method allocates interest and taxes to rental use based on rental use to total days in entire year.

Rental deductions other than tier 1 expenses are limited to gross rental revenue. When limited, deduct tier 1 expenses first, then tier 2, and finally tier 3 expenses.

The Tax Court justifies its allocation method by emphasizing that interest expense and property taxes accrue over the entire year regardless of the level of personal or rental use. Taxpayers generally choose the approach most beneficial to them. Determining which method is more favorable for a particular taxpayer depends on the taxpayer’s facts and circumstances, as discussed below. What is deductible if you rent part of your dwelling unit but not the whole thing, such as a Page 14-19

single bedroom or perhaps a basement of a two-level single-family residence? The taxpayer is required to divide the total expenses between those that are attributable to the rental portion versus the personal-use portion. Expenses that relate only to the rented portion of the dwelling unit (e.g., carpet or painting expenses for that room only) do not have to be prorated between rental and nonrental use. The two most common methods of prorating shared expenses between rental and nonrental use are the method based upon the number of rented rooms divided by the number of rooms in the dwelling unit or using the square footage of the rental room divided by the total square footage of the dwelling unit. Under either method, the taxpayer multiplies each category of shared expenses by the allocation percentage determined under the selected allocation method to arrive at the expenses allocated to determine the rental expenses of the dwelling unit.

What if: Suppose the Jeffersons rented a spare bedroom in their principal residence for the entire 2024 tax year. The spare room measures 300 square feet, and the total square footage of their residence is 3,000 square feet. Assume that the Jeffersons incur the following expenses during the 2024 tax year: Description Amount

| Column 1 | Column 2 |
| --- | --- |
| Repairing the drywall and repainting the spare room | $ 1,000 |
| Replacing a broken window in the spare room | 450 |
| Property taxes (entire residence) | 2,500 |
| Mortgage interest (entire residence) | 12,000 |
| Utilities (entire residence) | 2,000 |

What is the amount of rental expenses allocated to the spare room assuming the Jeffersons are using the square footage method to allocate expenses? Answer: $3,100($1,450 + $1,650). The $1,450 of expenses for the wall/repainting repair and the broken window are exclusive to the spare room and thus are allocated to the spare bedroom. The property taxes, mortgage interest, and utilities are expenses of the entire home and thus must be prorated between the spare bedroom and the remainder of the home. The allocation percentage for the spare bedroom is 10% (calculated as 300 square feet for the spare room/3,000 square feet for the entire dwelling unit). As a result, $1,650 of the total $16,500 of the entire home ($2,500 + $12,000 + $2,000) would thus be allocated to the spare room.
