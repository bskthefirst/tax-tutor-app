Incentive stock options Relative to NQOs, the granting of ISOs is less common. ISOs are sometimes referred to as statutory or qualified stock options. If the employee holds ISOs for at least two years after the grant date and the employee holds the stock received from exercising ISOs for at least one year after the exercise date, the employee gains two important tax benefits.20 First, the realized gain on the bargain element is deferred for regular tax purposes until the stock is subsequently sold.21 Second, the character of the gain on the subsequent stock sale is recognized as long-term capital gain rather than as ordinary income. Holding the number of options granted constant, employees generally prefer ISOs to NQOs due to the potential tax benefits for ISOs. The employee’s basis in shares acquired through exercising ISOs is equal to the exercise price paid and the holding period for the shares begins on the exercise date. If the employee meets the required holding period requirements (described above) with respect to the ISOs, the employee will recognize a long-term capital gain on the difference between the sales price and the employee’s basis in the shares upon subsequent disposition.

### Example 12-9

Assume that Julie exercises her ISOs when the employer stock price is $20 per share (see Exhibit 12-3). On the exercise date, Julie acquires 5,000 shares of TPI stock and pays TPI the exercise price of $5 per share. What are the tax consequences to Julie on the exercise of her ISOs? Answer: $0. There are no regular tax consequences on the ISO exercise because Julie held the shares at least two years after the grant date and one year after the exercise date. Julie’s realized gain is the bargain element of $75,000 [5,000 shares × $15 ($20 market price less $5 exercise price)], which is deferred until Julie sells the stock. What is Julie’s basis in the 5,000 shares she acquired through ISOs? Answer: $25,000 is the regular tax basis (5,000 shares acquired × $5 per share exercise price). What if: Suppose that five years after Julie exercised her ISOs and acquired 5,000 TPI shares with a basis of $5 per share, she sold all of her shares for $25 per share. What are the amount and character of the gain she will recognize on the sale? Answer: $100,000 long-term capital gain, calculated as follows:

| (1) Amount realized (sale proceeds) | $125,000 | (5,000 × $25) |

When an employee holding an ISO fails to meet either of the holding periods—holding the options for at least two years after the grant date and holding the stock for at least one year after the exercise date—the sale of stock is classified as a disqualifying disposition, and the bargain element (i.e., sales price minus exercise price) is taxed at the time of the stock sale as ordinary income—essentially the same treatment as an NQO but recognized on the date of the stock sale rather than the exercise date. Thus, when holders of ISOs execute same-day sales, or hold the shares for less than one year after exercise, they forgo the potential tax benefits of deferring the gain and having the bargain element taxed at lower capital gains rates—but they also eliminate the investment risk they would otherwise have to bear by holding the stock long enough to meet two-year-from-grant-date and one-year-from-exercise holding period requirements.
