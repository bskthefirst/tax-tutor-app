If the employer’s stock appreciates so that the stock price exceeds the exercise price, the options are valuable, and the employee will almost certainly exercise the options. The exercise of the option to purchase the employer’s stock requires the payment of cash equal to the exercise price. In certain circumstances, an employee may engage in a cashless exercise or same-day sale. A Page 12-10 cashless exercise is a technique that uses a brokerage firm to borrow funds in the amount of the exercise price (plus any associated brokerage fees) and then a portion of the sales proceeds is used to pay back the borrowed funds. A same-day sale typically does not require the employee to pay for the stock, but rather allows them to receive cash in the amount of the difference between the fair market value of the stock and the exercise price. Three primary reasons exist for a cashless exercise or same-day sale. First, the employee may not have enough cash on hand to cover the cost of exercising the options. Second, the employee may not have enough cash on hand to cover the future tax liability from exercising the options. Third, because the employee bears investment risk with respect to the employer stock, the employee might want to diversify their investment portfolio by selling the employer’s stock and investing in other stocks.

Employee Tax Considerations for Stock Options Stock options are classified for tax purposes as either nonqualified stock options (NQOs) or incentive stock options (ISOs). There are three important dates for employee stock options: the grant date, vesting date, and exercise date. While all three of these dates have economic consequences, only the exercise date has tax consequences to the employee. The employee’s tax consequences on the exercise date depend on whether the employee is exercising NQOs or ISOs.

---

Nonqualified options Most employer stock options are NQOs. When NQOs are exercised, an employee reports ordinary income equal to the bargain element on the shares of the employer stock acquired (as if they had sold the stock)—whether they hold the shares or sell the shares immediately.19 The employee’s basis in the newly acquired shares is equal to the fair market value of the shares on the exercise date. Thus, the basis includes the exercise price plus the ordinary income the taxpayer recognizes on the exercise (i.e., the bargain element) and the holding period for the shares begins on the exercise date. When the employee eventually sells these shares, the employee will recognize a long- or short-term capital gain, depending on the holding period, on the difference between the sales price and the employee's basis in the shares.
