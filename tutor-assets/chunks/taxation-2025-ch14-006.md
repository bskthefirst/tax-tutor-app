## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

A Nonowner Can Be an Owner Deducting home mortgage interest paid as acquisition indebtedness generally requires that the home be legally owned by the taxpayer claiming the deduction. However, the term owner not only is defined to be the person listed on the property deed but, in certain circumstances, also can be a taxpayer found to be an “equitable owner” under state law. John Wainwright was friends with a homeowner in Bethesda, Maryland, for over 35 years. In 2006, Wainwright and his friend refinanced the friend’s mortgage in order to make certain renovations to the home. Wainwright became a co-borrower along with his friend on the mortgage. However, the ownership deed to the home was not altered and only his friend remained listed as the legal owner of the home. Wainwright and his friend lived in the friend’s home during 2010 and 2011. Although Wainwright did not own the home, he was legally obligated as a co-borrower to repay the loan. Wainwright paid

---

all of the mortgage payments and maintained the property during the tax years in question. The IRS denied the home mortgage deduction claimed by Wainwright because Wainwright did not legally own the home. However, despite the lack of actual ownership, the Tax Court considered various state law factors to determine if Wainwright was still an “equitable owner” such that he had the benefits and burden of owning the property. Key factors used included the duty to maintain the property, risk of loss, obligation to pay taxes, and other charges as well as the right to use the property. Based upon the various state law factors analyzed, the Tax Court ruled that Wainwright was an equitable owner, and because he paid the mortgage, he was entitled to deduct the home mortgage interest deduction during the tax years in question. Source: John Wainwright v. Comm’r, T.C. Memo 2017-70.

### Limitation on Acquisition Indebtedness

## THE KEY FACTS

### Home Mortgage Interest Deduction

Deduction allowed for home mortgage interest on acquisition indebtedness.

Principal residence and one other residence.

Acquisition indebtedness

Proceeds used to acquire or substantially improve a qualified residence.

Limited to $1,000,000 if incurred prior to 12/16/2017.

Limited to $750,000 if incurred after 12/15/2017.

For purposes of determining the deductible amount of mortgage interest expense, the amount of acquisition indebtedness is limited to a fixed amount based on when the indebtedness originated. For acquisition indebtedness incurred after December 15, 2017, the amount of acquisition indebtedness is limited to $750,000 ($375,000 for married taxpayers filing separately). For acquisition indebtedness incurred before December 16, 2017, the amount of acquisition indebtedness is limited to $1,000,000 ($500,000 for married taxpayers filing separately), even if the debt is refinanced after December 15, 2017.10 When a taxpayer has both acquisition indebtedness incurred before December 16, 2017, and acquisition indebtedness incurred after December 15, 2017, the $750,000 ($375,000) limit is reduced (not below zero) by the acquisition indebtedness incurred before December 16, 2017. The higher $1,000,000 limit continues to apply to any debt incurred after December 15, 2017, to refinance debt incurred prior to December 16, 2017, to the extent the amount of the refinancing does not exceed the amount of the pre–December 16, 2017, existing debt. This exception for the refinancing of debt will not apply after (1) the expiration of the term of the original debt or (2) the earlier of the expiration of the first refinancing of the debt or 30 years after the date of the first refinancing.

### Example 14-6

What if: Assume that in January of 2024, the Jeffersons purchased a home in Chicago costing $900,000 by making a down payment of $100,000 and taking out an $800,000 loan secured by the home. During the year, the Jeffersons paid $24,000 of interest on the mortgage. How much of the interest are the Jeffersons allowed to deduct? Answer: $22,500($24,000 × $750,000/$800,000). Interest on $750,000 of $800,000 is deductible because the acquisition indebtedness was incurred after December 15, 2017, so the acquisition indebtedness is limited to $750,000 for purposes of determining their deductible mortgage interest. What if: Assume that in December of 2023, the Jeffersons purchased a home in Chicago costing $800,000 by making a down payment of $100,000 and taking out a $700,000 loan secured by the home. During 2024, the Jeffersons paid $21,000 of interest on the mortgage (and no principal). In addition, on July 1, 2024, the Jeffersons borrowed $20,000 through a loan called a “home equity loan” by the bank (the loan was secured by the home) and they paid $600 of interest on this loan in 2024. The Jeffersons used $15,000 of the proceeds from this loan to finish the basement in their home, and they used $5,000 of the proceeds as a down payment on a new car. How much home mortgage interest can the Jeffersons deduct in 2024? Answer: $21,450[$21,000 + $450($15,000/$20,000 × $600)]. The Jeffersons can deduct all $21,000 of interest on the $700,000 loan because the entire loan is acquisition indebtedness. However, because only $15,000 of the $20,000 “home equity loan” is acquisition debt for tax purposes ($15,000 of the $20,000 was used to substantially improve the home), the Jeffersons can deduct interest on only $15,000 of the loan. Thus, the Jeffersons can deduct $450 of interest on the additional $20,000 loan ($15,000/$20,000 × $600), and the remaining $150 of interest is nondeductible personal interest. What if: Assume that on December 1, 2017, the Jeffersons purchased a home in Chicago costing $900,000 by making a down payment of $100,000 and taking out an $800,000 loan secured by the home. During 2024, the Jeffersons paid $39,000 of interest on the mortgage. How much of the interest are the Jeffersons allowed to deduct? Answer: $39,000. All of the interest is deductible because the acquisition indebtedness was incurred before December 16, 2017, and it is under the $1,000,000 limit. What if: Assume that on December 1, 2017, the Jeffersons purchased a home in Chicago costing $900,000 by making a down payment of $100,000 and taking out an $800,000 loan secured by the home. In June of 2025, the Jeffersons refinanced the remaining $770,000 balance of the loan to get a lower interest rate. Are the Jeffersons allowed to deduct all the interest on the refinanced loan?

---

Answer: Yes. The Jeffersons can deduct all of the interest on the refinanced loan even though the balance exceeds $750,000 because the original acquisition indebtedness occurred before December 16, 2017, when the acquisition debt limit was $1,000,000.
