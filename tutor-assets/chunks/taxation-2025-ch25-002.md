## THE KEY FACTS

### Common Features of the Unified Transfer Taxes

Property transfers, whether made by gift or at death, are subject to transfer tax using a progressive tax rate schedule.

The applicable credit exempts cumulative transfers of up to $13.61 million.

Each transfer tax allows a deduction for transfers to charities and surviving spouses (the charitable and marital deductions, respectively).

Another common feature of the integrated transfer taxes is the applicable credit. This credit, Page 25-3 previously known as the unified credit, was enacted in 1977, and it applies to both the gift tax and the estate tax. The credit is designed to prevent the application of transfer tax to taxpayers who either would not accumulate a relatively large amount of property transfers during their lifetime and/or would not have a relatively large transfer passing to heirs upon their death. The value of cumulative taxable transfers a person can make without exceeding the applicable credit is called the applicable exclusion amount. We refer to this amount as the exemption.

Exhibit 25-2 presents the exemption for the estate and gift taxes since 1986. Except for the period between 2004 and 2010 (inclusive), the amount of the exemption has been the same for both the estate and gift taxes. Beginning in 2011, the exemption was set at $5 million and indexed for inflation. In 2018, Congress increased the exemption from $5 million to $10 million (adjusted for inflation) for the years 2018 through 2025. The exemption is scheduled to revert to $5 million (as adjusted for inflation) in 2026.1 The exemption significantly reduces the likelihood that the transfer tax will apply to most citizens. For example, according to the Center for Disease Control (CDC) there were over 3.4 million deaths reported in the United States, but according to the IRS SOI only about 3,400 taxable estate tax returns were filed in 2020.

The value of the applicable credit depends on both the transfer tax rates and the exemption that apply in the year of the transfer. Because the transfer tax rates can change over time and because the exemption changes with inflation adjustments, we can best calculate the applicable credit by tracking the amount of the exemption and converting it into the applicable credit using the current tax rate schedule. Consequently, the amount of the applicable credit for a transfer made in 2011 is calculated using the rate schedule in effect currently regardless of the amount of the applicable credit actually claimed in 2011. The exemption is $13.61 million for transfers made in 2024. Under the tax rate schedule for Page 25-4 2024, the applicable credit on the $13.61 million exemption is $5,389,800. A third common feature of the unified tax system is the application of two deductions. Each transfer tax provides an unlimited deduction for charitable contributions and a generous marital deduction for transfers to a spouse. The marital deduction allows almost unfettered transfers between spouses. In contrast to the income tax where a married couple can elect to file a single joint return, both individuals are required to file separate transfer tax returns. A final important feature of the transfer taxes is the valuation of transferred property. Property transferred via gift or at death is valued at fair market value. While fair market value is simple in concept, it is very complicated to apply. For purposes of the transfer taxes, fair market value is defined by the willing-buyer, willing-seller rule as follows: The price at which such property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell, and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts.2

Fair market value is determined based on the facts and circumstances for each individual property. Determining value is relatively simple for properties that have an active market. For example, stocks and bonds traded on exchanges or over the counter are valued at the mean of the highest and lowest selling prices on the date of the transfer. Unfortunately, the valuation of many other properties, especially realty, is very difficult. The large number of court cases resolving contentious values testifies to the difficulties in applying the willing-buyer, willing-seller rule.
