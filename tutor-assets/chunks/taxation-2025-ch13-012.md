## THE KEY FACTS

### Traditional IRAs

2024 deductible contribution limit:

Lesser of $7,000 plus a catch-up contribution for taxpayers who have reached 50 years of age by year-end.

The catch-up contribution is $1,000

Contributions for the year may not exceed the taxpayer’s earned income.

If taxpayers participate in employer-provided plan:

For 2024, deduction limit phased out for single taxpayers with MAGI between $77,000 and $87,000, and for married filing jointly taxpayers with MAGI between $123,000 and $143,000.

Special rules if one spouse is covered by plan and the other is not.

Deductible contributions to IRAs are for AGI deductions. The maximum deductible contribution for a taxpayer in 2024 depends on the taxpayer’s age, as follows: $7,000 plus a catch-up contribution for taxpayers who have reached 50 years of age at year-end.22,23 The catch-up contribution is $1,000.24

The deductible contribution limit may be further restricted depending on the following factors: The taxpayer’s participation in an employer-sponsored retirement plan. The taxpayer’s filing status. The amount of the taxpayer’s earned income.

The taxpayer’s modified AGI (MAGI). For purposes of determining the deductible amount of traditional IRA contributions, MAGI is the taxpayer’s AGI disregarding the IRA deduction itself and certain other items.25

Unmarried taxpayers not participating in an employer-sponsored retirement plan may deduct IRA contributions up to the lesser of: $7,000 ($8,000 including the $1,000 catch-up contribution for taxpayers at least 50 years of age at year-end). Earned income.

Earned income generally includes income actually earned through the taxpayer’s efforts, Page 13-20

such as wages, salaries, tips, and other employee compensation, plus the amount of the taxpayer’s net earnings from self-employment. Alimony income is also considered earned income for this purpose.

### Example 13-15

In the year she graduated from college, Tina earned $25,000. She earned $5,000 in salary working part time while she was in school, and she earned $20,000 in salary working for CBA from September through December. To get an early start saving for retirement, she contributed $5,000 to a traditional IRA [recall that Tina is not eligible to participate in CBA’s 401(k) retirement plan until January of next year]. How much of this contribution may Tina deduct? Answer: $5,000. Because Tina is not participating in an employer-sponsored retirement plan during the current year, she may deduct the full $5,000 contribution as a for AGI deduction, reducing her AGI from $25,000 to $20,000.

For unmarried taxpayers who actively participate in an employer-sponsored retirement plan, the deduction limits are the same as for those who do not participate, except that the maximum deduction is phased out based on the taxpayer’s MAGI, as follows: No phase-out if MAGI is equal to or less than $77,000. Proportional phase-out of full limit for MAGI between $77,000 and $87,000. [The phase-out percentage is computed as follows: (MAGI − $77,000) divided by ($87,000 − $77,000). For example, if MAGI is $79,000, the taxpayer loses 20 percent of deduction limit.] Full phase-out (no deduction) if MAGI is equal to or greater than $87,000.

Married taxpayers can make deductible contributions to separate IRAs (i.e., an IRA for each spouse) subject to limitations. If the married couple files jointly, before any AGI-based phase-out amounts (discussed below), the maximum deduction for the spouse with the higher amount of earned income is the same as it is for unmarried taxpayers [$7,000 ($8,000 if at least 50 years of age at year-end) or earned income if it is less]. However, the maximum deduction for the spouse with the lower amount of earned income is limited to the lesser of: 7,000 ($8,000 including the $1,000 catch-up contribution if this spouse is at least 50 years of age

The contribution to the lesser-earning spouse’s IRA is called a spousal IRA. The money in the account belongs to the lower-earning spouse no matter who earned the funds for the contribution. If a spouse is an active participant in an employer’s retirement plan and the couple files jointly, the maximum deduction for that spouse’s contribution is phased out based on the couple’s MAGI as follows: No phase-out if the couple’s MAGI is equal to or less than $123,000. Proportional phase-out of full limit for MAGI between $123,000 and $143,000 (for example, if the couple’s MAGI is $125,000, the couple loses 10 percent of deductible contribution limit). Full phase-out (no deduction) if MAGI is equal to or greater than $143,000.

If one spouse is an active participant in an employer’s retirement plan and the other is not Page 13-21

and the couple files jointly, the deduction for the spouse who is not an active participant is phased out based on the couple’s MAGI as follows (the phase-out applies whether the spouse is the higher- or lower-earning spouse): No phase-out if the couple’s MAGI is equal to or less than $230,000. Proportional phase-out of full limit for MAGI between $230,000 and $240,000. Full phase-out (no deduction) if MAGI is equal to or greater than $240,000.

Married taxpayers who file separately may also make deductible contributions to an IRA. The maximum deduction is the lesser of: $7,000 ($8,000 including the $1,000 catch-up contribution if taxpayer is at least 50 years of age at year-end). The taxpayer’s earned income.
