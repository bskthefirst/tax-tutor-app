### Example 9-5

Ramon employs Joan, an arborist who specializes in trimming trees and treating local tree ailments. Joan generates a great deal of revenue for Green Acres, but Joan is over 60 and suffers from MS. In November, Ramon paid $720 in premiums to purchase a “key employee” term life insurance policy on Joan’s life. The policy would pay Green Acres a $20,000 death benefit if Joan passes away during the next 12 months. Can Ramon deduct the life insurance premium? Answer: No. Ramon cannot deduct the $720 premium on the life insurance policy because the life insurance proceeds from the policy are tax-exempt.

---

What if: Suppose Ramon paid $720 in premiums to purchase a life insurance policy on Joan’s life, but the policy allows Joan to name her beneficiary. The policy would pay Joan’s beneficiary a $20,000 death benefit if Joan dies in the next 12 months. What amount of life insurance policy premium can Ramon deduct? Answer: $720. In this scenario, Ramon can deduct the entire premium of $720 as a compensation expense because the benefit of the policy inures to Joan (she names the beneficiary), and not to Green Acres.

Personal Expenditures Taxpayers are not allowed to deduct personal expenses unless the expenses are “expressly” authorized by a provision in the law.17 While the tax laws do not expressly define what constitutes a personal expense, the statute identifies “personal, living, or family expenses” as nondeductible examples. Hence, at a minimum, the costs of food, clothing, and shelter are assumed to be personal and nondeductible. Of course, there are the inevitable exceptions when otherwise personal items are specially adapted to business use. For example, taxpayers may deduct the cost of uniforms or special clothing they purchase for use in their business if the clothing is not appropriate to wear as ordinary clothing outside the place of business. However, when the clothing is adaptable as ordinary clothing, the cost of the clothing is a nondeductible personal expenditure.

### Example 9-6

Ramon spent $500 to purchase special coveralls that identify his landscaping service and provide a professional appearance. How much of the cost for the clothing can Ramon deduct as a business expense? Answer: $500. While the cost of clothing is inherently personal, Ramon can deduct the $500 cost of the coveralls because, due to the design and labeling on the coveralls, they are not suitable for ordinary use.

Many business owners, particularly small business owners such as sole proprietors, may be Page 9-8 tempted to use business funds to pay for items that are entirely personal in nature. For example, a sole proprietor could use the business checking account to pay for family groceries. These expenditures, even though funded by the business, are not deductible. Educational expenses constitute another exception. Expenditures made by a taxpayer for business education, including tuition and books, may be motivated by business aspirations. However, educational expenditures are not deductible as business expenses unless the taxpayer is self-employed and the education maintains or improves skills required by the individual in an existing trade or business. Education expenses necessary to meet minimum requirements for an occupation are not deductible. For example, tuition payments for courses to satisfy the education requirement to sit for the CPA exam are not deductible. These courses would qualify the taxpayer for a new trade or business and would be nondeductible even if the courses improved their skills in an existing trade or business.

## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

When is the cost of an MBA deductible? Ariana graduated in 2012 with a liberal arts degree in Spanish and French and began working as a translation services coordinator for Inline. This job required Ariana to work with customers by developing price quotes and project proposals as well as editing and proofreading manuscripts and managing translation teams. She also managed the budget for each of her projects. During 2014, Ariana enrolled as a part-time student in an MBA program at UCLA. However, Ariana was not reimbursed for her tuition because her employer only reimbursed expenses for foreign language classes. Ariana left Inline in May 2016 and began an internship at Mattel, Inc. (Mattel), as a global consumer insights intern. After completing the internship, Ariana was hired by Mattel as a senior consumer insight analyst, and she subsequently received her degree in 2017. Starting in 2014, Ariana deducted the tuition and expenses associated with her MBA as an unreimbursed employee business expense. Education expenses are deductible if the education maintains or improves skills required by the individual in their trade or business or meets the express requirements of the employer. However, the IRS disallowed the deductions after discovering that Mattel does not require an MBA for Ariana’s position and arguing that the UCLA classes qualified Ariana for a new trade or business. The Tax Court concluded that Ariana’s education expenses were not deductible by employing a “commonsense approach” that compared the tasks and activities Ariana was qualified to perform for Inline with those she was qualified to perform for Mattel. Judge Carluzzo observed that there was “no doubt some of the courses might have refined and improved the skills necessary for petitioner’s employment with Inline, insofar as a foundation in accounting, finance, and management could be helpful to anyone involved in the operation of a business. However, petitioner’s MBA studies were more specifically oriented towards the job for which she eventually left Inline.” Many of those courses were related to data analysis and prepared her to orchestrate and analyze online survey data and Ariana acknowledged that “she would not have felt comfortable making certain decisions required in her job with Mattel had it not been for her MBA courses.” Hence, the court held that Ariana’s education expenses were not deductible. Source: Ariana K. Uchizono, T.C. Summary Opinion 2023-21.

Mixed-Motive Expenditures Business owners in general, and owners of small or closely held businesses in particular, often make expenditures that are motivated by both business and personal concerns. These mixed-motive

expenditures are of particular concern to lawmakers and the IRS because of the tax incentive to disguise nondeductible personal expenses as deductible business expenses. Thus, deductions for business expenditures accompanied by personal benefits are closely monitored and restricted. The rules for determining the amount of deductible mixed-motive expenditures depend on the type of expenditure. The most common restrictions determine the deductible portion of mixed-motive expenditures for entertainment, meals, travel and transportation, and the use of property for both business and personal purposes.
