Once all the long-term losses have been used to offset long-term gains, any remaining long-term losses may be used to offset net short-term gains (if any). If, instead, long-term gains remain after using all long-term losses, net short-term losses may be used to offset the remaining long-term gains, beginning with the highest taxed gains, until the losses are exhausted or no gains remain. The gain remaining is called a net capital gain (net long-term capital gains in excess of net short-term capital losses). At this point in the process, two possible scenarios emerge: Only gains remain or only losses remain in the groups. The remaining capital losses are treated as discussed above. The tax on the remaining gains is determined as described in the next section.

Example 7-8 shows the netting process using the simplified process when taxpayers also sell long-term capital assets and recognize gains subject to the 25 percent and/or 28 percent capital gains rates.

Assuming Jeb did not sell any other capital assets during the year, he Page 7-16 recognizes an overall capital gain of $143,000. What is (are) the maximum tax rate(s) applicable to this gain? Answer: $95,000 of gain is subject to a 0/15/20 percent maximum rate and $48,000 of gain is subject to a 25 percent maximum rate, computed as follows: Jeb first sorts the gains and losses into short-term and long-term categories, resulting in a ($3,000) net short-term capital loss and long-term amounts totaling $146,000. Jeb will next sort the long-term amounts into gains and losses. The gain from Stock A and the loss from Stock B are placed in the 0/15/20 percent category, the gain from the gold coins is placed in the 28 percent category, $50,000 of the gain from the sale of the rental home is placed in the 25 percent category, and the remaining $70,000 gain from the rental home is placed in the 0/15/20 percent category. The sum of the gains and losses in each category results in a 28 percent net gain of $1,000, a 25 percent gain of $50,000, and a net $95,000 gain in the 0/15/20 percent category.

Jeb then uses the $3,000 net short-term capital loss to offset the $1,000 gain in the 28 percent category (the highest taxed gain). ## THE KEY FACTS

### 25 Percent and 28 Percent Rate Capital Gains

Certain gains from the sale of depreciable real property held long term are taxed at a maximum rate of 25 percent (unrecaptured §1250 gain).

Gains from collectibles and from qualified small business stock not held for five years are taxed at a maximum rate of 28 percent.

When taxpayers have 25 percent or 28 percent rate capital gains, the netting process requires that losses be applied to higher-rate groups before lower-rate groups.

When taxable income without long-term capital gains exceeds the maximum 15 percent rate amount, 25 and 28 percent capital gains are taxed at their maximum rate. Otherwise, applicable capital gains rates must be adjusted to reflect their inclusion in lower ordinary income rate brackets.
