## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

Take the Money and Run! In its report “How America Saves 2023,” Vanguard reports that the most common employer match for employer-sponsored defined contribution plans is $.50 for every dollar contributed by the employee on the first 6 percent of the employee’s income. When your employer is willing to give you money, you should find a way to take it if you can. Nevertheless, Vanguard’s report indicates that for the 2022 calendar year, 31 percent of employees participating in defined contribution plans did not contribute enough to receive the full employer match. The report also indicates that 17 percent of participating employees contributed exactly enough to receive the full match but did not contribute beyond that and 52 percent of participating employees contributed more than enough to receive the full match.

Contribution Limits THE KEY FACTS

---

Traditional Defined Contribution Plans: Contribution Limits 2024 employee contribution limit to 401(k) account:

$23,000 plus a “catch-up” contribution of $7,500 for employees 50 years old by year end).

2024 overall contribution limit for the sum of employee and employer contributions to employee’s 401(k) account:

Lesser of $69,000 plus employee’s catch-up contribution or 100 percent of employee’s annual compensation.

For 2024, employee contributions to the employee’s 401(k) account are limited to $23,000 plus an additional “catch-up” contribution of $7,500 for taxpayers who reach age 50 by year-end.7,8 While the limit on employee contributions is $23,000 ($30,500), the contribution limit on the sum of employee and employer contributions to an employee’s defined contribution account(s) is the lesser of (1) $69,000 plus the employee’s “catch-up adjustment” or (2) 100 percent of the employee’s compensation for the year.9 For example, if an employee under age 50 contributes the maximum $23,000 to their employer-sponsored 401(k) plan in 2024, the employer’s contribution would be limited to $46,000($69,000 − $23,000).10 Because of this limit on employer contributions, highly compensated employees may not be able to maximize the employee contribution and also receive the full employer match available to other employees.

### Example 13-4

Before Jason retires at the end of 2024, he continues to contribute to his CBA-sponsored 401(k) account. CBA matches employee contributions on a two-for-one basis up to 5 percent of the employee’s salary. Jason is 72 years old at the end of the year; he earned a salary of $700,000 during the year and contributed $30,500 to his 401(k) account. How much will CBA contribute to Jason’s 401(k) account? Answer: $46,000 [$76,500 (Jason is 50+ years old) − $30,500 (Jason’s contribution)]. Without the limitation, CBA would have contributed $70,000($700,000 × 5% × 2) to Jason’s account. What if: Assume Jason contributed $25,500 to his 401(k) account. What would CBA contribute to Jason’s 401(k)? Answer: $51,000 [$76,500 (Jason is 50+ years old) − $25,500 (Jason’s contribution)]. Note that this is a better deal for Jason because $76,500 ends up in his 401(k) account just as it would if he were to contribute $30,500. In this what-if scenario, however, Jason contributed only $25,500 instead of $30,500.

Vesting When employees contribute to a defined contribution plan, they are fully vested in the accrued benefit from those contributions (employee contributions plus earnings on employee contributions). However,

---

employees vest in the accrued benefit from employer contributions (employer contributions plus earnings on employer contributions) based on the plan’s vesting schedule. When they do not maintain separate accounts for the employee contributions and the employer contributions (one account has both employee and employer contributions plus earnings), the accrued benefit from employee contributions is calculated by multiplying the total accrued benefit in the account by the ratio of employee contributions to total contributions (employee plus employer) to the account.11 The accrued benefit from employer contributions is the difference between the total accrued benefit and the accrued benefit from employee contributions. The minimum vesting requirements for defined contribution plans are a bit more accelerated than the five-year cliff and seven-year graded vesting schedules applicable to defined benefit plans. For defined contribution plans, employers have the option of providing three-year cliff or six-year graded vesting. These minimum vesting schedules are presented in Exhibit 13-2.

After-Tax Cost of Contributions to Traditional (non-Roth) Defined Contribution Plans THE KEY FACTS Traditional Defined Contribution Plans: Vesting and After-Tax Cost Employee must vest to be entitled to employer contributions (and earnings on those contributions) using:

3-year cliff vesting schedule.

6-year graded vesting schedule.

Employee generally contributes before-tax amounts (contributions are deductible).

After-tax cost is before-tax contribution minus tax savings from contribution.

Employer-sponsored traditional defined contribution plans include all plans except Roth 401(k) plans (discussed below). Employees effectively deduct their contributions to traditional defined contribution plans (except for catch-up contributions that are treated as Roth 401(k) contributions) for income tax purposes because contributions are removed from their taxable salary. Contributions are still subject to FICA tax, however.12 Consequently, the after-tax cost of a contribution to a traditional defined contribution plan is the contribution amount minus the tax savings generated by the deduction from the contribution.
