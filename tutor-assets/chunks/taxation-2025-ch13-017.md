## TAXES IN THE REAL WORLD

How to Turn a Retirement Nest Egg into a Golden Egg Peter Thiel, founder of PayPal, used a Roth IRA as a vehicle to create an enormous amount of wealth that will never be taxed. An estimate from an article published online in June 2021 puts the balance in his Roth IRA at around $5 billion. So, how did he do it? Let’s go back to 1999 when it got started. In 1999, single taxpayers with income under $110,000 could contribute up to $2,000 annually to a Roth IRA. Thiel’s income for the year was just over $73,000, so he was able to contribute to a Roth IRA. Thiel used the funds contributed to the Roth IRA to purchase founders’ shares in PayPal. More specifically, he acquired 1.7 million shares in PayPal for $1,700. That’s only $.001 per share. After 1999, Thiel did not contribute any more funds to his Roth IRA. In 2002, the year eBay purchased PayPal, Thiel sold the PayPal shares in his Roth account and, by the end of the year, his Roth IRA was worth about $28.5 million. From there, Thiel used the cash in the account to make other investments, including shares in other companies such as Palantir and Meta (formerly Facebook), before they went public. By the end of 2008, Thiel’s Roth IRA was worth about $870 million and, as noted above, has grown to about $5 billion. Assuming he leaves funds in his Roth IRA until April 11, 2027, when he turns 59½ years of age, he will be able to withdraw the funds tax free. Thiel’s big advantage is that, as an “insider,” he was able to buy shares in a private company at what appears to be something significantly below market value. And he’s not the only one benefiting from these types of valuations. While undervaluing assets acquired in a retirement account is typically not allowed, valuation can be a subjective determination. In any event, Congress has previously considered legislation to restrict the taxpayers’ ability to accumulate large amounts of wealth in retirement accounts. Some prior proposals include provisions that are designed to prevent taxpayers from stuffing undervalued assets into Roth IRAs, requiring taxpayers with Roth IRA balances over $5 million to take distributions from the Roth IRA over time, and limiting taxpayers’ ability to convert from traditional retirement accounts to Roth IRAs. A wealth tax proposal has also been proposed. Under this proposal, a wealth tax would be imposed on households with a net worth of over $50 million. We will have to wait and see when and if these types of tax provisions will become law. In the meantime, for some taxpayers, their Roth IRA is like a golden goose.
