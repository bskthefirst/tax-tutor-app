Here are some common examples of permanent book–tax differences for a C corporation: 1. Interest Income: C corporations may earn interest income from various sources, such as savings accounts or investments in bonds. While this interest income is typically reported on the corporation’s financial statements as part of its book income, certain types of interest income may be tax-exempt or receive special tax treatment under the Internal Revenue Code. For example, interest from municipal bonds is often tax-exempt at the federal level. 2. Dividend Income: Dividend income received by a C corporation from investments in other corporations may be subject to a dividends-received deduction, which reduces the taxable income. This results in a permanent book–tax difference because the financial accounting income includes the full amount of the dividend income. 3. Life Insurance Proceeds: When a C corporation receives life insurance proceeds due to the death of an insured key employee, the amount of the insurance proceeds may be taxable for book purposes but tax-free for income tax purposes, depending on certain conditions and limits. 4. Income from Tax-Exempt Bonds: Income generated from investments in tax-exempt bonds, such as municipal bonds, is generally tax-exempt at the federal level. However, this income is typically included in the book income. 5. Meal and Entertainment Expenses: For book purposes, C corporations may fully deduct certain meal and entertainment expenses as business expenses. However, the tax code imposes limits on the deductibility of these expenses, resulting in a permanent difference between book and tax income. 6. Capital Gains and Losses: Gains and losses from the sale of capital assets, such as stocks or real estate, may be taxed at different rates than ordinary income. Additionally, there may be preferential tax rates for certain capital gains, resulting in a permanent difference between book and tax income. 7. Non-Deductible Fines and Penalties: Fines and penalties imposed by regulatory authorities or governments are generally not tax-deductible. These expenses, while included in book income, are typically not deductible for income tax purposes.
